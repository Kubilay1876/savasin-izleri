Fronts / El Alamein

NORTH AFRICA

Battle of El Alamein

A battle fought in the Egyptian desert, about a hundred kilometres from Alexandria. Rommel's advance towards the Suez Canal ended here.

01 Background

Since 1940 the war in North Africa had swung back and forth between Libya and Egypt. In the summer of 1942, the German-Italian forces under Rommel pushed the British deep into Egypt.

The British managed to hold on at El Alamein. With the sea to the north and the almost impassable Qattara Depression to the south, the front was a narrow strip, and neither side could outflank the other.

02 The battle

Montgomery, who took command of the British Eighth Army in August, reorganised his forces. Together with troops from Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and India, he built up a clear advantage in men, tanks and guns.

On the night of 23 October, after a massive bombardment by hundreds of guns, infantry cleared paths through the minefields. The photograph on this page was taken the next day; the photographer is known to have staged the scene.

When the attack began, Rommel was in Germany on sick leave; the general commanding in his place died of a heart attack on the second day of the battle, and Rommel rushed back to the front. After twelve days of gruelling fighting, the Axis defence collapsed on 4 November.

03 Outcome and significance

Despite Hitler's order not to retreat, Rommel pulled his forces back to the west. The threat to the Suez Canal and the Middle East was removed.

A few days later, American and British forces landed in Morocco and Algeria. Caught between two fires, the Axis forces in North Africa surrendered in Tunisia in May 1943.
